# B6 drawing, expression and soft-light lock

Apply to **every character and environment**, not just one traced face. **B6 works best in portrait (9:16). Sample 1, the portrait V44 version of “The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower”, is the user-confirmed primary, strict acceptance reference** for drawing, limb weight, meme acting, soft light, spatial depth, camera movement and rhythm. Inspect its moving reference as well as `assets/b6/reference/approved-character-v21b.png` and the matching `paint/` layers before new work. Sample 2, Hong Kong V90, is a secondary binding/engineering reference; its finer arms do not replace Sample 1's drawing standard. Rejected polished/vector trials are not style alternatives.

## Shape and brush

- White head: slightly flattened, tilted, asymmetric contour; uneven jaw and imperfect hair mass. Do not substitute an exact circle/ellipse or a generic round-head stickman.
- Meme acting: short heavy upper lids, low pupils, uneven brows, off-center mouth and simplified irregular teeth. Eyes/mouth/brow geometry expresses suspicion, greed, surprise, disappointment, excitement and recovery. Use readable changes tied to the current beat, not one smug smile for the whole film. Keep gaze on the partner/prop and let the reaction follow the perceived event.
- Unified body: simple slightly crooked clothed torso, sparse dark limbs and simple hands/shoes; let the garment silhouette express the selected fit, shoulders and sleeves. The head and body share ink pressure, paint softness and level of detail. Identity can change skin/hair/clothes/proportions; it cannot revert to a clean vector body under a painted head. Victor's short brown side part has no twin hair tails; other characters are not required to wear Victor's hair or red coat.
- Stick-limb priority: keep garment volume concentrated on the torso and preserve readable single-stroke, pressure-varying ink arms with small dark simplified hands. Match their relative weight to Sample 1 portrait: its arms are visibly heavier than Sample 2's fine lines. Compare unobscured arms at matched head size; record the sampled stroke-to-head proportion and pressure variation for each identity/view instead of prescribing an unmeasured universal pixel width. Do not turn exposed arms into paired outlines filled with skin color or modeled elbows/wrists. When the setting allows, prefer lightweight short or narrow sleeves so the arms remain legible; express era/trend through hair, color blocks, collar, pattern and a few accessories rather than inflated shoulders, bulky rolled sleeves or extra layers. If the story needs long sleeves or heavy clothing, design their silhouette deliberately while retaining the sparse stick-figure read. A watch is a small mark on the ink wrist, not a reason to introduce anatomical forearms.
- Ink: selective pressure variation, lighter upper/lit edges and heavier jaw/shadow edges, a few deliberate repasses/breaks. Slightly off-model strokes have shape logic. Do not add uniform zigzags, random scratches, jittering contour noise or the same roughness everywhere.
- Pigment: broad matte colored masses, restrained local variation and a few visible brushed boundaries. Texture lives in the asset's local coordinates so it moves with the artwork; no frame-by-frame noise swimming over the face. Keep painted sampled ink for moving limbs instead of perfect SVG strokes and circular joint caps.

## Era, place and trend direction

When the user specifies an era, region or cultural setting, make it visibly shape **clothing, hairstyle/headwear, accessories and scenery together**. B6 locks the drawing grammar, not a generic wardrobe or the bundled Paris cast. A date in the prompt, old props and a warm filter do not constitute period art direction.

Before painting, save a concise era/look brief with the cast and environment records: requested year range and place, story role/social milieu, relevant period visual references, chosen silhouette and fashion cues, hairstyle, character-relevant accessories, palette, and architectural/shopfront/transport/interior cues. If no era is requested, follow the story's setting; do not invent a retro theme. Resolve routine choices from the brief rather than adding a new mandatory approval step.

- **Choose a recognizable, expressive version of the period.** By default, favor its distinctive fashion/trend vocabulary over an average, unobtrusive outfit. Deliberately amplify a characteristic silhouette, hair shape, accessory scale or color combination enough to read at the intended shot size. Keep occupations, personality and story context legible; a clerk, delivery worker and fashionable customer should not all receive the same outfit.
- **Design the person as a whole.** Specify cut/fit/layering, collar/sleeve/hem shape, hair volume/parting or headwear, and a few purposeful accessories. Select and omit details intentionally. Hair and props must work with the oversized meme head and sparse stick limbs. Bold design comes from readable shapes, not realistic fabric rendering, anatomical legs or dozens of tiny decorations.
- **Make the place equally specific.** Architecture, storefront typography, street furniture, transport, interior fixtures and everyday objects should reinforce the same time/place. Distribute distinct period landmarks through the continuous wide set so a lateral move reveals new local detail. Generic buildings with one old car, or generic cafe seating with one vintage object, need further design.
- **Choose a coherent local look, not a decade costume pile.** Use relevant period images to check candidates; distinguish country/city, early/late decade and subculture or class. Do not treat American and Hong Kong 1990s fashion as interchangeable or assume a present-day retro revival is authentic. Exaggeration can amplify period shapes; it should not introduce anachronisms, flatten everyone into a stereotype or copy a specific celebrity/brand by default. Respect an explicitly requested understated, documentary or fixed-costume treatment.

### Period-read review

Review each concept at the intended wide-shot scale as well as face/body crops:

- With the year label, subtitles and explanatory caption hidden, do clothing, hair, accessories and the environment collectively suggest the requested setting? The broad read should not depend on a small VHS cassette, sign or taxi alone.
- What is the principal silhouette/fashion statement, and is it still visible when the character is small? Compare the main cast's silhouettes and personalities; avoid interchangeable plain shirts and generic hair.
- Does every selected cue belong to the same time/place/story milieu? Record uncertain references rather than claiming exact historical accuracy.
- Does the stronger era design still use B6's imperfect ink, matte paint, simple limbs, meme expression and soft light? Reject a fashion upgrade that turns the cast into polished realistic humans or an unrelated illustration style.
- Do the exposed single-stroke arms match Sample 1 portrait's relative ink weight and pressure variation at matched head size, including the supporting cast? If sleeves swallow the arm or a flesh-filled forearm appears, simplify the garment and limb before extending the concept to other views.

If the era is weak, repair the specific costume/hair/accessory or set design before extending animation. Preserve unaffected accepted material. Record this review separately from actual user acceptance; listing era keywords or passing the code validator does not prove the picture works.

## Lighting and finish

Start from the right paint values. Face is near-white with a soft pale-violet underside; dark ink stays legible. Use warm diffuse room light, softer local occlusion at chin, sleeve, hand/prop, shoe/ground and counter contact. Warm windows/lights may spill a little into nearby edges; lift only the relevant highlights. Preserve shadow color and midtone separation.

Plan wall/floor light and character shading together. A character needs a grounded soft shadow whose size/opacity matches contact and elevation; a paper needs a small contact shadow. Move the world light with the world, not with a screen overlay. Matte hair/clothes should not acquire plastic specular highlights.

If a finishing pass is useful, compare at matched framing/exposure: a restrained highlight-only bloom, soft shoulder and warm/cool balance may support the paint. Review first frame, a lit face, a dark room and a camera transition. Reject crushed ink, washed-out faces, clipped windows, heavy uniform blur, muddy blacks and a filter used to conceal bad drawing. Do not promise the original uses a particular filter/software without evidence.

## Reusable still-art brief

Adapt this style atom to the approved identity/scene and attach the approved whole-character and face/body references when the configured still tool supports them:

> A deliberately imperfect painted meme cartoon for layered 2D animation. Asymmetric slightly flattened white head, heavy short eyelids and uneven brows, off-center expressive mouth, sparse slightly crooked body and simple single-stroke stick limbs with the relative ink weight of the approved Sample 1 portrait, avoiding Sample 2's finer wire-like arms. The face and clothes share the same loose pressure-varying ink and broad matte paint. Selective broken and repassed contours, pale violet soft underside shadows, warm diffuse light and restrained spill at bright edges. Keep it scrappy and funny, readable at small size, with stable character identity. No polished vector outline, perfect circular head, glossy 3D rendering, ornate illustration, random scratch overlay or disconnected head/body styles.

Add the specific identity, exact view/expression/pose, layer/transparent-background requirements and story-specific setting. For an era/place brief, expand the actual choices into each applicable still prompt: `period/place + character role + distinctive clothing silhouette/cut/colors + hair/headwear shape + purposeful accessories + local scene/transport/interior cues + intended degree of exaggeration`. Do not reduce this to “1990s retro” or “period appropriate”; the character and scene must receive concrete design directions. Repeat approved identity/look choices across views while changing only the requested pose/expression. Do not include motion in a still prompt and expect it to create a rig. For scenery, request a continuous very wide painted world with distinct regions, usable floor, deep layers and enough vertical overscan for the planned cameras; include a world layout. A portrait delivery can still use a very wide scene asset.

### Environment-led prompt additions

For an environment or crowd-led beat, state the actual shot subject and whether the protagonist is absent. Avoid automatically attaching a full-character prompt to every background request. Specify distinct connected regions, their layout, ground/horizon, near/middle/far relationships, intended lighting and the coverage needed by the planned camera. Keep the approved B6 ink and soft painted finish; borrowing another style's spatial planning does not import its contour weight or cel-shadow treatment.

Example brief to adapt, not a fixed opening: `A continuous wide 1990s Hong Kong street in the approved B6 painted meme style. Left: narrow shopfronts and a small delivery area; center: a readable pavement, crossing and traffic lane; right: the story's tea restaurant entrance, with its destination clearly recognizable. Layered foreground signs/awning edges, middle street and distant upper facades, consistent horizon and usable ground. Uneven pressure-varying ink, broad matte paint, soft warm light and pale cool shadows. No protagonist or oversized featured portrait. Preserve complete artwork behind the planned foreground occluders and room for the specified camera route.`

Then specify the asset contract: a clean environment plate omits people/vehicles that need independent motion; request or reuse separate period-consistent pedestrian, customer and vehicle layers with their needed views. A final establishing shot may contain a crowd even though its base plate is empty. If only static extras are requested, describe their placement and activities explicitly and record that they cannot move independently until separated. Missing views or layers remain asset gaps; this brief does not authorize generation or spending.

The [Sample 1 acceptance matrix](b6-sample-1-acceptance.md#drawing-paint-and-light) makes drawing, relative stroke, material/light and final-output observations independently reviewable. Texture density must follow the focal hierarchy: face, clothes and scenery need not carry equal grain. Preserve localized pigment/ink marks through motion; global warm tint, blur and grain cannot establish matching paint or light. Inspect native-size encoded crops, not only a source still or low-resolution contact sheet.

## Asset handoff and review

For each cast card save identity, view IDs, expression IDs, layer paths/hashes, opaque-pixel shoulder/hip/head pivots, fixed bone lengths, grip sockets, occluders and supported poses. Test front/three-quarter/side/back at the same scale. Never invent a missing angle by narrowing the front head. If an expression is a replacement bitmap, preserve head/hair silhouette and light; if it is a separate face layer, match the sampled ink and facial surface.

Review whole figure and close face/body/arm crops beside Sample 1 portrait at matched head size; compare light and brushwork as well as stroke weight. Keep the character scale and camera transform uniform: changing zoom, thickening a finished screen image or changing only the head cannot establish a matched limb style. New identities may change costume, hair and personality while retaining this shared drawing grammar. Apply the period-read review when an era/place is specified. A second character must pass the same drawing and period review. Record accepted/pending/rejected separately. The bundled cast is usable reference artwork, not proof that a newly generated identity already matches.
