## Cold open (0:00 – 0:22)

> **VO:** You did not see most of today. Nobody does.
>
> Your brain takes in about eleven million bits of information a second. It can consciously
> process about fifty.
>
> Everything else gets deleted, smoothed over, or invented.
>
> And the strangest part? The memories it keeps are not a recording of what happened. They are
> a story it tells you, about you, that gets more flattering every time you retell it.

`[VISUAL: figure walks across frame, stops. Eleven million tiny dots stream past it; only a`
`handful land. The rest fade. Cut to a framed "memory" hanging on a wall that changes slightly.]`

---

## Stakes (0:22 – 1:05)

> **VO:** This sounds like a fun fact for a dinner table. It is not.
>
> It means the arguments you are sure you won, you probably did not win.
> It means the reason you are stuck in the same year, again, is not that nothing has changed.
> It is that you keep telling yourself the same edited version of it.
>
> Your brain is not a camera. It is a defence lawyer.

`[VISUAL: figure stands in a courtroom, tiny, defending itself to a giant judge made of light.]`

---

## The thing everyone believes (1:05 – 3:10)

> **VO:** Here is what almost everyone believes about memory.
>
> Memory is a filing cabinet. Things happen, they get stored, and later you take them out and
> read them. If you have a bad memory, the filing is messy.
>
> That model is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that quietly runs your life.
>
> In 1974, two researchers, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer, showed people a film of a car
> crash and then asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" into each other. Another
> group was asked how fast they went when they "contacted" each other.
>
> Same film. Different verb.
>
> The "smashed" group estimated nine miles per hour faster. A week later, they were more likely
> to remember broken glass that was never in the film.
>
> One word changed what they saw. Not what they remembered seeing. What they saw.

`[VISUAL: filing cabinet opens, paper flies out. Cut to two cars, one word "SMASHED" /`
`"CONTACTED" held by the stickman, then a broken-glass shard that dissolves.]`

> **VO:** And it is not only other people. In 1999, Christopher Chabris and Daniel Simons asked
> people to count basketball passes and told them to ignore everything else. In the middle of
> the clip, a person in a gorilla suit walks through the scene, stops, and beats its chest.
>
> Half the viewers never saw the gorilla.
>
> Half. Of people. Looking directly at a gorilla.

`[VISUAL: stickman counting, focused. Gorilla walks behind. A counter ticks. Cut to "50%" in`
`huge type. Then the gorilla waving at the viewer, ignored.]`

> **VO:** You are not the exception. You are the person quietly missing the gorilla, and then
> confidently explaining what happened in the room.

---

## The turn (3:10 – 5:05)

> **VO:** So if memory is not a recording, what is it?
>
> It is a reconstruction. Every time you remember something, you are not opening a file. You are
> rebuilding the scene from fragments, expectations, and the last time you told the story.
>
> Which means every retelling changes it. Memory is not damaged by time. It is edited by use.
>
> Psychologists call the reshaping "reconsolidation". The metaphor that holds up is a document
> that opens slightly differently each time you save it.
>
> Now add the second glitch: your brain keeps the version of events that protects your identity.
> It is called self-serving bias. If you win, it was skill. If you lose, it was the traffic, the
> manager, the weather, the algorithm.

`[VISUAL: memory as a wall of photographs. The figure touches one and it re-renders. Second`
`glitch: two versions of the same scene — one where the figure is a hero, one a fool. The hero`
`version glows and gets bigger.]`

> **VO:** And the third glitch, the one that decides whether this year looks like last year.
>
> Your brain is a prediction machine. It is not trying to see reality accurately — it is trying
> to spend as little energy as possible. So it predicts, then only checks the parts it expects.
>
> When the prediction is wrong, it does not update your whole world. It files the exception
> under "weird day".
>
> That is why you can have a hundred small signs that something needs to change, and remember
> one Tuesday when everything was fine.

`[VISUAL: the figure walks the same loop. Every lap the loop gets smoother and the scenery`
`drops into lower resolution. On the last lap the ground is a single flat line.]`

---

## The mechanism (5:05 – 7:00)

> **VO:** Here is the useful part. You cannot switch these off. But you can starve them.
>
> Three habits, and none of them requires believing in yourself.
>
> **One. Write the version you would not say out loud.** Not a journal. One sentence, at night:
> "Today I was wrong about ___." The point is not honesty. The point is that you have now made a
> competing memory you will read tomorrow.
>
> **Two. Retell an old story to someone who was there.** Watch what they remember that you do
> not. It is deeply uncomfortable. It is also the cheapest correction available to a human being.
>
> **Three. Change one variable, not everything.** The loop is not a lack of willpower. It is a
> prediction the brain finds cheap. Same commute, same feed, same conversations. Give it one new
> input a day — a different route, a different seat, one conversation with someone outside your
> usual five. The prediction starts costing more than the alternative.

`[VISUAL: three cards appear in sequence. Each one is acted out: pen on paper, two figures`
`disagreeing, one figure stepping off a familiar path. The wristband catches the light on each`
`gesture.]`

> **VO:** The third one is the one people skip, because it is the one that actually changes the
> year. Your brain will not update a story it never has to re-read.

---

## Payoff (7:00 – 8:05)

> **VO:** So come back to the opening.
>
> You did not see most of today. That is not a bug you can fix by paying more attention. Attention
> is a spotlight, not a floodlight.
>
> What you can fix is what you do with the seconds you do see.
>
> The brain you are using right now is not a witness. It is a narrator, and it is very good at
> making the last chapter fit the story so far.
>
> Which means the question is not "what happened to me". The question is:
>
> "What story am I currently protecting — and what would I have to admit to stop protecting it?"
>
> Answer that one honestly, and this year already looks different from last year.

`[VISUAL: the figure stands at the loop's edge. Behind it, the wall of memories. It does not`
`break the loop — it steps sideways, and the loop keeps turning without it.]`

---
